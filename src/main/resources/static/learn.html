F&O Field Guide

Futures and options explained from the very first idea to a planned, sized and written-down trade. Every term is explained the first time it appears, and again in the glossary at the end.

Read this first

SEBI studied more than one crore individual F&O traders. Between FY22 and FY24, 93% of them lost money, around ₹2 lakh each on average once costs were included (SEBI study, September 2024). This guide explains how F&O works and how to control risk. It cannot remove the risk, and nothing in it is a recommendation to buy or sell.

01

The market you trade in

Who is involved when you press Buy, and what each of them does.

Segments

A broker account is split into segments, and each one is switched on separately.

  • Cash or equity: you buy actual shares. They arrive in your demat account one working day later (called T+1 settlement).
  • F&O (futures and options): you trade contracts based on an index or a stock. You usually never own the shares. Activating this segment needs income proof, such as a bank statement or tax return.
  • Currency and commodity: the same contract ideas applied to the rupee, gold, crude oil and so on (commodities trade mainly on MCX).

When it trades

The normal session runs from 9:15 AM to 3:30 PM IST, Monday to Friday, except exchange holidays. Prices move fastest in the first fifteen minutes and in the last hour.

In plain English

In the cash market you pay for shares and wait a day to receive them. In F&O nothing is delivered day to day. Instead, every evening your account is credited with the day's profit or debited with the day's loss. That daily settling-up is called mark-to-market.

02

What a derivative is

A contract whose price comes from something else.

A derivative is an agreement between two people whose value depends on the price of something else, called the underlying. If the underlying moves, the contract's value moves with it. In Indian F&O the underlying is either a stock index or a single stock.

  • Indices: NIFTY 50, NIFTY BANK, FINNIFTY, MIDCPNIFTY and NIFTY NEXT 50 on NSE; SENSEX and BANKEX on BSE.
  • Stocks: about 200 large, heavily traded companies. The list is revised a few times a year.

Why derivatives exist

  • Hedgers want protection. A fund holding ₹100 crore of shares can buy put options so a market fall hurts less.
  • Speculators want to profit from a view on direction while putting up less money than buying the shares outright.
  • Arbitrageurs profit from small price gaps, for example when a stock's future is priced too far above the share.

Every trade needs two sides. When you speculate, the person on the other side is often a hedger or a professional who does this all day.

Rules that change

Since September 2025, NSE contracts expire on Tuesdays (monthly contracts on the last Tuesday of the month) and BSE contracts on Thursdays. Weekly expiries exist for only one index per exchange: NIFTY on NSE and SENSEX on BSE. When expiry falls on a holiday it moves to the previous trading day. Lot sizes are revised periodically so that each index contract is worth at least ₹15 lakh. Always check the exchange's current circulars before trading.

At any time three monthly contracts trade: the near month (expiring soonest, the busiest), the next month and the far month.

03

Futures

An agreement to buy or sell later at a price fixed today.

A futures contract fixes a price today for a trade that settles on expiry. Both sides are bound to it. The buyer is long and gains if the price rises. The seller is short and gains if it falls. You can sell a future without owning the shares, which is how traders bet on a fall.

Worked example

ABC Ltd trades at ₹2,000 and its lot size is 500 (an example figure). One futures contract controls ₹2,000 × 500 = ₹10,00,000 of shares. You don't pay ₹10 lakh. You deposit margin, roughly 15–25% of the contract value, say ₹2,00,000.

If ABC rises 1% to ₹2,020, you gain ₹20 × 500 = ₹10,000, which is 5% of your margin. If it falls 1%, you lose the same ₹10,000. That magnification is called leverage, and it works in both directions.

Margin, and what happens every evening

The exchange works out how much one contract could lose on a bad day and asks for that up front. This initial margin has two parts: SPAN margin (the exchange's worst-case estimate) and exposure margin (an extra buffer). Your broker blocks it in your account when you open the trade.

Then, every evening, the contract is marked to the day's closing price and the difference is settled in cash.

Watch out

If losses eat into your margin, the broker asks you to add money. That is a margin call. If you don't, the broker can close your position at whatever the market price is, usually at the worst moment.

Futures price versus share price

A future usually trades a little above the share price. The gap is the basis. Above the share price is a premium, below it a discount. The premium mostly reflects interest for the days left, called the cost of carry. As expiry approaches the gap shrinks to zero, and on expiry day the future settles at the share's closing price.

Rollover, expiry and settlement

  • Rollover: to hold a view beyond expiry, you close this month's contract and open next month's. The price difference between the two is your cost.
  • Index futures are settled in cash at the final settlement price.
  • Stock futures are settled by physical delivery. If you're still long at expiry you must pay the full contract value and take the shares; if you're short you must deliver shares. Most traders exit before expiry day.

Open interest, and what it tells you

Volume counts contracts traded today. Open interest (OI) counts contracts still open, opened and not yet closed. Rising OI means fresh money is entering; falling OI means positions are being closed. Read together with price, it shows who is in control.

04

Options

The right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price.

Think of an option like insurance. You pay a small amount, the premium, for a right that you may or may not use. The person who sells you that right, the option writer, keeps the premium and takes on the obligation.

  • A call option gives the right to buy the underlying at a fixed price. You buy calls when you expect a rise.
  • A put option gives the right to sell at a fixed price. You buy puts when you expect a fall, or to protect shares you own.
  • The fixed price is the strike price. Every option has a strike, an expiry and a lot size.

What a premium is made of

Every premium has two parts. Intrinsic value is what the option would pay if exercised right now. Time value (also called extrinsic value) is everything else: what buyers pay for the chance that price moves further before expiry.

Exercise and settlement in India

  • Indian options are European style: they can only be exercised on expiry day. Before that, you exit by selling the option in the market, which is what almost everyone does.
  • At expiry, ITM options are exercised automatically. Index options settle in cash.
  • Stock options settle by physical delivery. An ITM stock call held to expiry means you must buy the shares at the strike, paying the full value. Brokers raise margins sharply in expiry week and may close such positions themselves. Exit ITM stock options before expiry day.
  • An option exercised at expiry attracts a higher securities transaction tax on its intrinsic value than one sold in the market. Selling before expiry is usually cheaper.

Why most option buyers lose

Time value shrinks every day and falls fastest in the final week. A buyer needs the move to come quickly and be big enough to beat this decay. A seller collects it, but carries the risk of a large move.

05

Reading an option chain

The screen where every strike for one expiry is listed side by side.

An option chain lists all strikes for one underlying and one expiry. Calls are on the left, puts on the right, strikes in the middle. ITM rows are usually shaded.

  • LTP (last traded price) is the option's current premium.
  • OI is how many contracts are open at that strike. Chg OI is how much that changed today.
  • IV (implied volatility) is how big a move the market is pricing in. Higher IV means more expensive options.
  • Bid and ask (not shown) are the best prices buyers and sellers are offering. The gap between them is the bid-ask spread.

What traders read from it

  • A strike with very high call OI means many sellers expect price to stay below it, so it often acts as resistance (25,200 above). High put OI often acts as support (24,800 above). These are tendencies, not walls.
  • PCR (put-call ratio) is total put OI ÷ total call OI. Very high or very low readings show crowded positioning.
  • Max pain is the strike at which option buyers as a group would lose the most at expiry. Some traders watch it on expiry day. Treat it as background, not a signal.
Watch out

Trade only strikes with a tight bid-ask spread and healthy OI. In a thin strike you can buy at ₹12 and find the best bid is ₹10 a second later. That gap is lost the moment you enter, and again when you exit. The difference between the price you expected and the price you got is slippage.

06

The Greeks

Four numbers that explain why an option's price moves.

An option's premium changes for three reasons: the underlying moves, time passes, and expectations of volatility change. The Greeks measure each effect. Your broker's option chain shows them for every strike.

ΔDelta

How much the premium moves for a ₹1 move in the underlying. ATM options have a delta near 0.5, deep ITM near 1, far OTM near 0. Puts have negative delta.

A 0.5-delta call gains about ₹50 if NIFTY rises 100. Delta is also a rough guide to the chance of expiring ITM.

ΓGamma

How fast delta itself changes as price moves. Highest for ATM options close to expiry.

This is why expiry-day options can jump from ₹5 to ₹50, or collapse to zero, in minutes.

ΘTheta

How much value the option loses per day from time passing, all else equal. Shown as a negative number for buyers.

A theta of −12 means the premium drops about ₹12 a day. Sellers earn this; buyers pay it.

νVega

How much the premium changes for a 1-point change in implied volatility.

Options get pricier when fear rises and cheaper when it fades, even if price doesn't move.

Rho measures sensitivity to interest rates. For short-dated Indian options it barely matters.

Volatility: the hidden price driver

  • Historical volatility measures how much the price actually moved recently. Implied volatility (IV) is the move that option prices are currently assuming.
  • India VIX is a fear gauge built from NIFTY option prices. A VIX of 13 roughly means the market expects NIFTY to move about 13% over a year. When VIX jumps, all option premiums rise.
  • IV crush: before results, budgets or RBI policy, IV rises because a big move is expected. Once the news is out, IV collapses. A buyer can be right about direction and still lose money as the premium deflates.

Strategies to start with

Combine positions to match your view and cap your risk.

A strategy is two or more positions held together. The point of most strategies is to limit how much you can lose, often in exchange for limiting how much you can make. Positions that offset each other are hedged, and hedged positions need less margin.

Margin, costs and tax

What you put up, what you pay on every trade, and how profits are taxed.

Margin rules you'll meet

  • The full margin must be in your account before you trade. Brokers can no longer give extra intraday leverage beyond exchange margins.
  • Option buyers pay the full premium up front and nothing more.
  • Option sellers and futures traders have margin blocked, often ₹1 lakh or more per index lot. Hedged positions, such as spreads, need much less.
  • On expiry day, exchanges charge extra margin on short options, and some offset benefits stop. Expect higher blocks that day.

Worked example (illustrative)

Buy one NIFTY call lot of 75 at ₹150 and sell it at ₹160. Gross profit: ₹10 × 75 = ₹750. Charges: brokerage ₹40, STT ₹12, exchange charges ≈ ₹8, GST ≈ ₹9, stamp duty and SEBI fee under ₹1. Total ≈ ₹69, so you keep about ₹681. On small, frequent trades, charges take a big share. Rates shown are as of 2025; use your broker's charges calculator for exact figures.

Tax, in outline

  • F&O profits are treated as business income (non-speculative) and taxed at your income-tax slab rate.
  • Costs of trading (brokerage, data subscriptions, internet, a share of equipment) can be claimed as business expenses.
  • F&O losses can be set off against other income except salary, and carried forward for eight years if you file your return on time.
  • You file ITR-3. Depending on your turnover and profit, a tax audit may be required. F&O "turnover" for tax is calculated from your profits and losses, not from contract values. A chartered accountant will do this properly.

Managing risk

The part that decides whether you're still trading next year.

Professionals decide how much they are willing to lose before they think about how much they might make. The most common rule: risk no more than 1% of your trading capital on any single trade. With ₹5 lakh, that is ₹5,000. Ten losing trades in a row would cost about 10%, which you can recover from.

Rules that keep you in the game

  • Decide the stop before you enter, and place it. Never move a stop further away to avoid taking a loss.
  • Look for reward at least 1.5× the risk. Then you can be wrong more often than right and still come out ahead.
  • Set a daily loss limit, for example 2–3% of capital. Hit it and stop for the day.
  • Don't average down. Adding to a losing position turns a small mistake into a large one.
  • Avoid revenge trades. The trade right after a painful loss is usually the worst one of the week.
  • Respect events. Results, the budget, RBI policy and global news can gap prices past your stop overnight. Know the calendar.
  • Keep a journal. Write down every trade: setup, entry, stop, target, result, and what you'd do differently. After 50 trades your own data will teach you more than any course.

Orders and the trading day

How to place orders, and what a disciplined day looks like.

You also choose a product type. MIS (intraday) positions are closed automatically by the broker near the end of the day, typically between 3:15 and 3:25 PM. NRML (normal) positions can be carried overnight until expiry, with full margin.

A disciplined trading day

8:45 AM

Prepare. Check global markets and GIFT NIFTY, overnight news, the day's results and events, and the F&O ban list. Mark key levels on your watchlist.

9:15 AM

Open. Prices jump around as overnight orders hit. Let the first 5–15 minutes settle unless your plan is built for the open.

9:30 – 11:30

Trends form. The strongest stocks of the day usually show themselves here. Most clean setups appear in this window.

12:00 – 1:30

Lunch lull. Volume drops and moves get choppy. Fewer trades, smaller size.

2:30 PM

Last fresh entries. Less time left means less room for a trade to work, and expiry-day options decay fastest now.

3:15 PM

Square off. Close intraday positions yourself before the broker does it at market price.

3:30 PM

Close and review. Write up every trade in your journal while it's fresh.

F&O ban

Each stock has a market-wide position limit (MWPL). When open interest crosses 95% of it, the stock enters the F&O ban: nobody can open new positions, only reduce existing ones, until OI falls below 80%. Check the exchange's ban list every morning.

Reading price like a pro

The price-action terms used in the OB Terminal, explained.

Swing high / swing low

A peak with lower highs on both sides, or a trough with higher lows on both sides. These mark structure.

Break of structure

A candle closes beyond the latest swing high or low. A close counts; a wick poking through doesn't.

Order block

The last opposite-colour candle before a strong move that broke structure. Big orders were likely placed there, so price often reacts when it returns.

Fair value gap

A gap inside a fast move where three candles don't overlap. It shows the move was forceful.

Liquidity

Clusters of stop orders, usually just beyond equal highs or equal lows. Price is often drawn there before reversing.

VWAP

Volume-weighted average price for the day. Buyers above VWAP and sellers below it have the average participant on their side.

Z, S1, R1, R2

Z is the entry level in the zone. S1 is where the idea is wrong for a long (R1 for a short). R1 and R2 are targets ahead (S1 and S2 for a short).

R:R (reward to risk)

Distance to the target ÷ distance to the stop. 1:2 means you aim to make twice what you risk.

Strength

Today's move compared with the stock's normal daily range. A 2% move is huge for a calm stock and ordinary for a volatile one.

Breadth and sector rotation

How many stocks are up versus down, and which sectors money is flowing into. A setup that moves with the market and its sector has the wind behind it.

Ready-to-trade checklist

Tick every box before each trade. If one stays empty, don't take it.

Before your first real trade

  • Paper trade (record trades without real money) for at least 30 sessions, with a journal.
  • Start with defined-risk trades: bought options or spreads, not naked selling.
  • Trade one lot. Increase size only after 50 journaled trades show a positive result.
  • Keep a cash buffer on top of margin so a bad day doesn't force you out.

Before every trade

During and after

  • During: let the stop and target do their job. Trail the stop only if your plan said you would.
  • After: journal the result, a screenshot and a grade for how well you followed the plan, separate from whether you made money.
